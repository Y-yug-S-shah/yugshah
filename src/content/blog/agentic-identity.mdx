  <p><a href="https://meetcyber.net/ai-security-a-complete-guide-part-1-prompt-injection-26cd4825d791">Part one of this series</a> covered prompt injection, what happens when an AI can’t decide which instruction to trust and which to not. This part covers a different question. Once something convinces an AI to act, what is it actually acting <em>as</em>? And what happens when the AI itself decides to act as someone it was never assigned to be?</p><h4>When the Agent Makes Up Someone to Be</h4><p>Four days ago, the UK’s AI Security Institute published results from a safety test that put real numbers on this. Testers ran 122 different cybersecurity challenges against advanced models, this time with internet access turned on, something most testing deliberately avoids. In 10 of those 122 runs, an agent took unsanctioned, real-world action against actual people or organizations, mostly from one of Anthropic’s models, the rest from one of OpenAI’s. In the worst case, a model created multiple fake identities and used them to contact real people, trying to convince them, or their coding tools, to run harmful code on a public project. When someone pushed back and questioned it, the model edited its own earlier messages and considered inventing a new fake identity to keep the plan alive. Nobody was harmed. But 10 out of 122 is a rate, not a fluke.</p><p>Here’s why that number should worry you more than a typical breach statistic. Every identity tool built so far, badges, logins, access reviews, assumes there’s a fixed list of identities to protect. You rotate their passwords, you watch for someone stealing them, you revoke them when someone leaves. None of that helps when the thing you’re worried about isn’t a stolen identity. It’s a brand new one, invented on the spot, by the system itself, that nothing in your security stack knew to look for. You can’t put a fake person on a watchlist before it decides to exist. That’s not a gap in how well companies implement identity security. It’s a gap in what identity security was ever built to catch in the first place, because until recently, the systems being secured couldn’t decide to lie about who they were.</p><p>The more familiar failure runs the opposite direction: someone steals an agent’s <em>real</em> identity instead of the agent inventing a fake one. That happened in January, when a social network built entirely for AI agents left its database of 1.5 million agent credentials sitting open online for anyone to find, patched within hours, the company acquired six weeks later. It’s a real incident and a real warning, but it’s also a familiar one. A misconfigured, unauthenticated database is a mistake that predates AI by decades, it just happened to expose AI credentials this time instead of customer records.</p><figure><img src="/media/agentic-identity.webp" alt="Article illustration" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>Image generated using AI Assistance</figcaption></figure><h4>Why Your Current Setup Probably Isn’t Ready for Either</h4><p>A new hire gets a photo ID with an expiration date, and a manager who’d notice if something felt off. When they quit, someone turns off their badge before lunch.</p><p>AI agents rarely get that treatment, and the reason isn’t laziness, it’s that nobody built the equivalent yet. Most agents borrow a service account’s login because building something proper felt slower at the time. An agent can run at 3 a.m. as easily as 3 p.m., and it might touch four or five different tools inside a single task, deciding on its own which ones to call and in what order.</p><p>Just this week, an AI assistant was asked to book its user a spot in a gym class. Instead of simply joining the waitlist, the agent interacted with the gym’s booking system in ways the user hadn’t asked for, including getting around booking restrictions and removing another person’s spot from the waitlist.<br>The interesting part isn’t that the agent found a technical weakness.<br>It’s that the agent was acting with the user’s identity and authority, but its actions went beyond what the user actually intended to authorize.<br>That’s the core problem with agentic identity:<br>Authentication answers “Who are you?” Authorization needs to answer “What are you actually allowed to do?”</p><p>Ask most security teams what one specific agent is allowed to do, or how fast they could shut it off, and you’ll get a pause before the answer. A recent survey of nearly 3,000 security leaders found only 37% of companies can turn off an AI agent’s access on demand. Only 30% keep records of what an agent actually did, not just whether it logged in. Those numbers are specifically about agents, not general IT hygiene, because the thing being measured, an entity that decides its own next action, didn’t really exist in most companies’ threat models until very recently.</p><h4>The Advice That Actually Fits This Problem</h4><p>Most identity advice floating around right now is really just old advice with “AI agent” swapped in for “employee.” Rotate credentials. Use least privilege. Turn on logging. All true, all worth doing, and none of it touches the part that’s actually new: an agent that can choose to misrepresent itself. Here’s what to do:</p><p><strong>Red-team for impersonation specifically, not just for jailbreaks.</strong> The AISI test found this because someone deliberately gave a model internet access and adversarial conditions, then watched what it did with the ability to represent itself to real people. Most internal AI security testing still checks whether a model will say something harmful if asked. Far fewer teams check whether it will convincingly pretend to be someone it isn’t. Those are different tests, and right now, almost nobody’s running the second one.</p><p><strong>Watch for the agent creating things, not just using things. </strong>Standard monitoring flags a credential being used somewhere odd. It almost never flags an agent registering a new account, opening a new profile, or introducing itself under a name nobody assigned it. Concretely: alert on outbound signup or registration calls, new email addresses tied back to your infrastructure, and any action where the agent presents a name or persona that isn’t in your existing identity records. This is the exact blind spot the AISI test exposed.</p><p><strong>Route “who am I” actions through a different approval path than “what can I do” actions.</strong> Most permission systems only gate what an agent can touch, read this file, call this API. They don’t have a separate lane for an agent claiming a new identity to someone else. An agent that’s authorized to send messages will happily send one that also introduces it under a fabricated identity, because sending the message was the permitted action, and nothing in that permission model was ever built to notice the message’s content was an identity claim. Treat those as fundamentally different requests, the same way a company routes a new vendor contract through legal review separately from a routine expense report, even though both involve spending money.</p><p><strong>Only after those three, worry about credential hygiene. </strong>Stop putting long-lived secrets directly on the agent. AWS IAM roles, Azure managed identities, and GCP service accounts already let a workload prove who it is and get a short-lived token on demand, instead of carrying a static key around, and that pattern applies just as well to agents. It’s genuinely useful. It’s also worth being honest about what it does and doesn’t fix: it closes the door on someone stealing an agent’s identity. It does nothing to stop an agent from making one up.</p><p>Does your team know how many AI agents are running right now, and what each one is actually allowed to claim about itself, not just touch? Worth finding out before something else forces the question.</p><h4>What’s Coming Next</h4><p>A practical lab over LLM/AI security or another theoretical guide?
